📘 Resume Writing Guide

A short, practical guide to structuring your resume, quantifying your impact, and staying ATS-friendly — before you jump into the editor.

1. Resume structure that works

Most hiring managers skim a resume in 6–10 seconds before deciding whether to read further. A clear, predictable structure helps them find what they need fast:

  • Header — Name, target title/headline, and contact info (email, phone, location, LinkedIn).
  • Professional Summary — 2–3 sentences that frame who you are and what you bring.
  • Key Achievements (optional but powerful) — 2–4 standout, quantified wins pulled to the top.
  • Professional Experience — Reverse-chronological, with 3–5 bullets per role.
  • Skills — Grouped by category (e.g. Languages, Tools, Cloud, Soft Skills) rather than one long list.
  • Education & Certifications — Degree, institution, dates; certs with the year obtained.
Length rule of thumb Aim for one page if you have under ~10 years of experience, and no more than two pages otherwise. Use the editor's Page fit gauge to check as you write.

2. Writing a strong professional summary

Your summary is the first thing recruiters read — make it specific, not generic. A good formula:

[Role/title] with [X years] of experience in [domain/specialty]. Known for [core strength or achievement]. Looking to bring [specific skills] to [type of role/company].

✗ "Hardworking professional looking for new opportunities."

✓ "Backend engineer with 6+ years building scalable APIs and event-driven systems. Led a migration that cut infrastructure costs by 30%. Seeking a senior role focused on distributed systems."

3. Turning duties into achievements

Avoid simply describing what your job was — describe what you did and the result. A useful structure is:

Action verb + what you did + how / with what + measurable result.

✗ "Responsible for managing the sales team."

✓ "Led a team of 8 sales reps, restructuring territory assignments to increase quarterly revenue by 22%."

Start bullets with strong action verbs: Led, Built, Launched, Designed, Reduced, Automated, Negotiated, Migrated, Mentored. Avoid weak openers like "Responsible for" or "Helped with."

4. Quantifying your impact

Numbers make claims concrete and credible. When possible, include:

  • Scale — team size, number of users, records processed, budget managed.
  • Change — percentage improvement, time saved, cost reduced.
  • Speed — how quickly something was delivered relative to a baseline or deadline.

If you don't have an exact number, use a clearly labeled estimate only when you can explain how you arrived at it. Never invent precision: "approximately 20%" is safer than a made-up "21.7%." Keep supporting detail available for interviews.

5. Staying ATS-friendly

Many companies use an Applicant Tracking System (ATS) to parse resumes before a human ever sees them. To stay compatible:

  • Use standard section headings (Experience, Education, Skills) rather than creative alternatives.
  • Avoid text inside images, complex tables, or multi-column text boxes that some ATS parsers can't read reliably.
  • Include keywords from the job description naturally in your summary, skills, and bullets.
  • Save/export as a well-structured PDF or HTML rather than a scanned image.
  • Spell out acronyms at least once (e.g. "Model Context Protocol (MCP)") so both ATS keyword matching and human readers understand.

6. Tailoring for each application

A resume tailored to the specific role consistently outperforms a generic one. Before applying:

  • Re-read the job description and highlight 5–8 recurring keywords/skills.
  • Make sure your summary and top bullets reflect the most relevant of those keywords — genuinely, not by stuffing.
  • Reorder or trim bullets so the most relevant achievements for this role are near the top of each job entry.
Tip Save a "master" version with everything, then use Save HTML in the editor to create a tailored copy per application — the exported file is self-contained and easy to keep as an archive.

7. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Walls of text. Long paragraphs get skipped — use bullets.
  • Inconsistent formatting. Mixed date formats, fonts, or bullet styles look unpolished.
  • Listing every tool you've ever touched. Group and prioritize skills relevant to the target role.
  • No context for numbers. "Improved performance by 40%" is stronger with a baseline: "…reducing average query time from 5s to 3s (40%)."
  • Typos and broken links. Always proofread, and test any hyperlinks (portfolio, LinkedIn, certifications) before sending.
  • Overflowing the page. Keep an eye on the Page fit gauge in the editor — content that spills onto a second page can look unintentional if it's just a line or two.

8. Final checklist

  • ☐ Name, title, and contact info are correct and up to date
  • ☐ Summary is specific to the role you're targeting
  • ☐ Each bullet follows action → what → result, with numbers where possible
  • ☐ Skills are grouped and relevant to the target role
  • ☐ Formatting is consistent (dates, fonts, spacing)
  • ☐ No typos — read it out loud once before exporting
  • ☐ Fits on one page (or two, for senior/extensive experience)
  • ☐ Exported as PDF (and/or HTML backup) with a clear file name
